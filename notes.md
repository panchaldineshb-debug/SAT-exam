## 1. Math: Systems of Linear Equations & Mixture Problems

### Slope and Number of Solutions
For two linear equations written in standard form:
Line 1: A1*x + B1*y = C1
Line 2: A2*x + B2*y = C2

*   **No Solution (Parallel Lines):** The lines have the same slope but different y-intercepts.
    -   *Rule:* The ratio of the x-coefficients equals the ratio of the y-coefficients, but does not equal the ratio of the constants.
    -   *Formula:* A1 / A2 = B1 / B2 (and is not equal to C1 / C2)
*   **Infinitely Many Solutions (Same Line):** The two equations represent the exact same line.
    -   *Rule:* The entire second equation is a constant multiple of the first equation.
    -   *Formula:* A1 / A2 = B1 / B2 = C1 / C2
*   **One Solution (Intersecting Lines):** The lines have different slopes.
    -   *Rule:* The ratios of the coefficients are not equal.
    -   *Formula:* A1 / A2 is not equal to B1 / B2

### Graph Intersections on the Axes
*   If a system intersects at a point on the **x-axis**, the y-coordinate of the intersection is always **0** (x, 0).
    -   *Strategy:* Plug y = 0 into the first equation to solve for x. Then substitute this (x, 0) coordinate into the second equation to solve for any unknown constants.
*   If a system intersects at a point on the **y-axis**, the x-coordinate of the intersection is always **0** (0, y).
    -   *Strategy:* Plug x = 0 into the first equation to solve for y. Then substitute (0, y) into the second equation.

### Mixture Word Problems
*   *Setup:* Let x be the amount of the first solution and y be the amount of the second solution.
*   *Equation 1 (Total Volume):* x + y = Total Volume
*   *Equation 2 (Total Substance/Salt):* (Concentration 1)*x + (Concentration 2)*y = (Final Concentration)*(Total Volume)
*   *Strategy:* Use substitution. Isolate y in the first equation (y = Total Volume - x) and plug it into the concentration equation.

### Dilution Problems (Adding Pure Water)
*   *Concept:* Adding pure water (0% concentration) increases the total volume of the liquid but does not change the amount of the active substance (acid, salt, etc.).
*   *Formula:* (Initial Concentration) * (Initial Volume) = (Target Concentration) * (Initial Volume + Water Added)
    -   *Example:* Diluting 2 liters of 30% acid to a 10% concentration:
        0.30 * 2 = 0.10 * (2 + w)
        0.6 = 0.2 + 0.1w
        w = 4 liters of water to add.

## 2. Reading and Writing (RW)

### Cross-Text Connections (Comparative Passages)
*   **Identify Evidence Differences:** Pay close attention to what type of evidence each text relies on. For example, Text 1 might rely on "isolated fossils" while Text 2 relies on "group burials" (evidence of social grouping).
*   **Avoid Extreme or Unmentioned Claims:** Do not select options that claim evidence was "fabricated," "faked," or "scavenged" unless the text explicitly states so. The SAT prefers moderate, precise descriptions of logical limitations (e.g., "overlooks evidence").
*   **Evaluate Response Tone:** Ensure that the answer choice accurately reflects the relationship between the texts (agreeing, challenging, or refining).

### Standard English Conventions: Verb Tense & Aspect
*   **Future Perfect Continuous:** Used to describe an action that will continue up to a specific point of time in the future.
    -   *Key Markers:* A future time indicator (e.g., "**next year**") paired with a duration marker (e.g., "**for over six months**").
    -   *Structure:* **will have been + verbing** (e.g., *will have been monitoring*)
*   **Past Perfect:** Used to describe an action that occurred and was completed *before* another past action.
    -   *Structure:* **had + past participle** (e.g., *had sat*)
*   **Present Perfect & Present Perfect Continuous:** Used for actions that started in the past and continue to or have direct relevance in the present.
    -   *Key Markers:* Duration phrases such as "**over the past three decades**," "**for the last year**," or starting points like "**since last month**."
    -   *Structure:* **has/have been + verbing** (e.g., *have been monitoring*) or **has/have + past participle** (e.g., *has experienced*).
*   **Third Conditional (Hypothetical Past):** Used to express a hypothetical situation in the past and its imaginary result.
    -   *Structure:* **If + had + past participle, ... would have + past participle**
        -   *Example:* *If the team had secured funding earlier, they would have completed the trials by now.*

## 3. Session Notes & Key Traps (July 21, 2026)

### Math Execution & Precision Rules
*   **The Target Check Rule (Trig & Triangle Side Ratios):**
    -   When solving right triangle / similar triangle problems, double-check whether the question asks for the **opposite side**, **adjacent side**, or **hypotenuse**.
    -   *Example:* If sin(A) = 5/13, the ratio of opposite:adjacent:hypotenuse is 5:12:13. If scaled by factor k = 3, the opposite side (EF) is 5 * 3 = 15, whereas the adjacent side (DF) is 12 * 3 = 36. Always re-read the target variable!
*   **Grid-In Numerical Entry Protocol:**
    -   Never rush past question headers. Grid-in (Student-Produced Response) questions do NOT have multiple-choice letters (A, B, C, D). Always enter the exact calculated integer or decimal value (e.g., `21825`).
*   **Margin of Error & Projections:**
    -   *Minimum Projected Supporters:* (Sample Percentage - Margin of Error) * Total Population.
    -   *Example:* (52% - 3.5%) * 45,000 = 0.485 * 45,000 = 21,825.

### Reading & Writing Rules
*   **Avoid Out-of-Scope Concepts (Cross-Text Connections):**
    -   In Cross-Text Questions, eliminate any choice that introduces concepts, terms, or theories not explicitly supported by the text (e.g., introducing "long-term memory" when the text only discusses working memory resource allocation).
*   **Dangling Modifier Rule:**
    -   When a sentence begins with an introductory participial phrase (e.g., *"Hoping to reduce municipal carbon emissions..."*), the noun directly following the comma **must** be the entity capable of carrying out that action (e.g., *city council members*, NOT *bus lanes*).
*   **Participle Clause Punctuation:**
    -   A present participle clause at the end of a sentence expressing a result or consequence requires a single preceding comma before the participle (e.g., *...Staphylococcus bacteria, creating an...*). Never place a comma after the participle verb (`creating, an`), as that splits the verb from its direct object!

## 4. Re-Drill Session Notes & Key Traps (July 22, 2026)

### Math Execution & Precision Rules
*   **Target-Check Verification (Hypotenuse vs. Adjacent/Opposite):**
    *   *Trap:* Finding the correct scaling factor `k` but multiplying by the wrong side ratio (e.g., multiplying `k = 3` by hypotenuse `17` to get `51` instead of adjacent side `8` to get `24`).
    *   *Rule:* Take 5 seconds after solving the geometry/ratio to point at the question and confirm: "Did it ask for adjacent (XZ), opposite (YZ), or hypotenuse (XY)?"
*   **Maximum Projected Quantities (Margin of Error):**
    *   *Formula:* `Maximum Projected Quantity = (Sample Percentage + Margin of Error) * Total Population`
    *   *Example:* `(94% + 2.5%) * 28,000 = 0.965 * 28,000 = 27,020`.
*   **Linear Systems with No Real Solution (Standard Form):**
    *   *Rule:* For `A1*x + B1*y = C1` and `A2*x + B2*y = C2`, set `A1 / A2 = B1 / B2`.
    *   *Example:* `4 / b = -10 / 15` ➔ `4 / b = -2 / 3` ➔ `b = -6`.

### Reading & Writing Rules
*   **Participle Clause Punctuation vs. Sentence Fragments:**
    *   *Rule:* A present participle phrase (`-ing` phrase) expressing a result at the end of a main clause must be preceded by a **single comma** (`..., revealing a...`).
    *   *Fragment Trap:* Never separate a present participle result phrase with a period (`. Revealing a...`), as it creates an ungrammatical sentence fragment.
    *   *Semicolon Trap:* Never use a semicolon before a participle phrase (`; revealing a...`), because a semicolon requires complete independent clauses on both sides.

## 5. Advanced Sprint Notes & Key Traps (July 26, 2026)

### Math Execution & Precision Rules
*   **Exponential Decay Rate Trap (Base vs. Exponent):**
    *   *Rule:* In $M(t) = M_0 (b)^{t/k}$, the base $b$ represents the percentage remaining (e.g., $0.5$ means a 50% decrease). The denominator $k$ in the exponent represents the time it takes for that decrease to occur once.
    *   *Example:* $(0.5)^{t/14}$ means the mass decreases by 50% every 14 days.
*   **Target Check Rule (Vertex Form):**
    *   *Trap:* Finding the $x$-coordinate (time) of the vertex using $t = -b/(2a)$ but forgetting to plug it back into the equation to find the $y$-coordinate (maximum height). Always double-check what the question asks for!
*   **Grid-In Exponential Equations (Base Conversion):**
    *   *Rule:* To solve equations like $8^x = 16^{y+1}$, you must convert both bases to a common base (like 2) before setting exponents equal.
    *   *Example:* $8 = 2^3$ and $16 = 2^4$. So, $(2^3)^x = (2^4)^{y+1} \implies 3x = 4y + 4$.

### Reading & Writing Rules
*   **Cross-Text Connections (Extreme Language Trap):**
    *   *Rule:* Be highly suspicious of answer choices with extreme modifiers like "entirely failed," "always," or "any region." If a choice directly contradicts a conceded point in the text (e.g., Text says "undeniably increased... in certain regions", choice says "entirely failed... in any region"), eliminate it immediately.
*   **Appositives (Essential vs. Non-Essential):**
    *   *Rule:* If the noun preceding a name is a broad category (like "The American author"), the specific name is **essential** to identifying the subject. **Do not** use commas around essential appositives.
    *   *Correct:* The American author Toni Morrison wrote *Beloved*.
*   **Dangling Modifiers (The "Who is Doing It?" Trap):**
    *   *Rule:* An introductory modifying phrase (like "Floating silently in the ocean,") must be immediately followed by the noun that is actually performing the action (e.g., the anglerfish, NOT the lure).
*   **Complex Lists (The Super-Comma):**
    *   *Rule:* If an SAT list uses semicolons to separate later complex items (e.g., "...gear; and training..."), you MUST use a semicolon to separate all items in that list for parallel consistency.

## 6. Trap Resolution Sprint Notes (July 27, 2026)

### Math Execution & Precision Rules
*   **The Premature Rounding Trap (Grid-In):**
    *   *Rule:* Never round intermediate calculation steps on the SAT. Keep the long decimals in your calculator until the very final operation, and only round the final answer. Rounding early causes cascading errors that will make your final grid-in incorrect.
*   **Infinitely Many Solutions (Standard Form Systems):**
    *   *Rule:* For a system to have infinitely many solutions, the ratio of the coefficients must be completely equal across the board. 
    *   *Setup:* For $A_1x + B_1y = C_1$ and $A_2x + B_2y = C_2$, set up $\frac{A_1}{A_2} = \frac{B_1}{B_2} = \frac{C_1}{C_2}$. Use the constant ratio (e.g., $\frac{10}{5} = 2$) to solve for unknown variables.

### Reading & Writing Rules
*   **Extreme Language Alert (Persistent Trap):**
    *   *Rule:* Treat words like *entirely*, *banned*, *always*, and *never* like glowing red stop signs. The SAT almost always prefers moderate, defensible answers (e.g., "the model has limitations") over extreme absolutes.
*   **Rhetorical Synthesis Goal Check:**
    *   *Rule:* Always double-check the specific **goal** of a Rhetorical Synthesis question. If the prompt asks to *contrast* two things (e.g., humans vs. shrimp), you must choose the option that mentions *both* and explicitly shows their difference. An option that only describes one of the items is a decoy.
